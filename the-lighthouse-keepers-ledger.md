# The Lighthouse Keeper's Ledger

Every night for forty-one years, Elias climbed the spiral stairs of the Point Merrow lighthouse and lit the lamp. And every night, before climbing down, he wrote a single line in a leather ledger his father had left him: *the sea was kind*, or *a storm passed, no ships lost*, or simply *fog*.

The villagers thought it was habit. Elias knew it was a promise.

The ledger had begun in 1889, when his father wrote the first line after a wreck that took the *Cormorant* and eleven men. The keeper had been drinking that night. He never drank again, and he never stopped writing. The book passed to Elias in 1923, along with the stairs, the lamp, and the guilt.

Elias was not a drinking man, but he had his own night to atone for — a fog-bound trawler in his tenth year, when he'd misread the wind and the *Merrow Bess* had gone aground on the black rocks. Six men. He'd counted their names every night since, adding them silently to his father's eleven.

Then came the October storm of 1952.

It arrived without warning, a wall of water and wind that bent the marram grass flat and made the tower hum like a struck bell. The lamp turned. The fog signal bellowed. And through the rain-slicked window, Elias saw it: a fishing boat, small and desperate, driving straight toward the shallows.

He grabbed the ledger first — an old man's strange instinct — and shoved it inside his coat before hauling himself down the stairs and out into the screaming dark. He fired the line-throwing gun twice before the rope found the mast. One by one, four men came across the rocks, hands frozen, faces bloodied, alive.

The last one, a boy of maybe nineteen, stopped on the shingle and looked up at the tower.

"You kept the light on," the boy shouted over the wind. "All night. My grandfather sailed these waters — he said Point Merrow never failed."

Elias looked at him and saw the storm of 1935, the *Merrow Bess*, all of it stacked up behind the boy's salt-crusted face.

"Come inside," was all he said.

They drank tea while the storm wore itself out against the stone. In the morning, the sea lay flat and shining, forgiving in the way the sea is only after it has tried to take something. The boy shook his hand at the door.

"What's the boat called?" Elias asked.

"*Merrow Bess II*," the boy said. "My grandfather built her new. Said he'd keep the name going."

Elias stood in the doorway a long time after the boy had gone.

That night, he climbed the stairs one last time, wet coat still steaming by the stove. He opened the ledger and wrote the line he had been waiting his whole life to write:

*The sea took nothing. The Merrow Bess came home.*

He read it twice, closed the book, and for the first time in forty-one years, slept before dawn.

Six names. Eleven names. The sea kept its ledger too — but so did the keepers, and theirs balanced at last.
